For a century, load was the only dial we had. You picked a weight, counted reps, and hoped the stimulus matched the plan. Velocity-based training (VBT) adds a second dial: **how fast the bar actually moved.**

That matters because the same weight is a different stimulus on different days. A 150&nbsp;kg squat that flies up at 0.6&nbsp;m/s is a very different session from one that grinds at 0.3&nbsp;m/s — even though the number on the bar is identical.

## What velocity actually tells you

- **Readiness.** If your usual warm-up weights are moving slower than baseline, you're under-recovered today. The bar knows before you do.
- **Intent.** Training to move a submaximal load *as fast as possible* drives power adaptations that slow grinding reps can't.
- **Auto-regulation.** Instead of "5×5 at 80%", you stop the set when speed drops past a threshold. Volume adjusts to the athlete, not the spreadsheet.

## Where the cheap sensors fall short

The linear-position transducers used in labs are accurate but expensive. The wave of sub-$100 accelerometer clips and phone-camera apps democratized VBT — but they trade accuracy for price:

- Camera apps struggle with lighting and framing, and estimate rather than measure.
- Clip sensors drift on lateral bar movement and multi-joint lifts.

None of that makes them useless. It means you should treat their numbers as **relative** — trends against your own baseline — not absolute truth you can compare to someone else's setup.

## The takeaway

You don't need VBT to get strong. But if you already grind through fixed percentages, a velocity number is the cheapest way to find out whether today's plan matches today's body.
